Think about the spaces your central system has always struggled with: a garage workshop that bakes by July, an addition the ductwork never reached, a bonus room that runs too warm or too cold, or an older Kent home built without ducts in the first place. Those are exactly the jobs a ductless mini split was made for. There is no ductwork to leak or lose energy, because each wall-mounted head is fed by a line set that passes through a small hole in the wall. Inside the outdoor unit, an inverter compressor modulates to match the load, so the room holds within a fraction of a degree, quietly and on very little power. And when winter comes, it runs as a true cold-climate heat pump.

Many disappointing mini splits were doomed on paper, not on the wall. The classic error is oversizing: a head too big for its room cools in a hurry, shuts off before it pulls out the humidity, and hunts all day. Kent HVAC sizes every zone to its real load instead. On multi-zone systems we also check that the outdoor unit's capacity makes sense across the heads it feeds and that every line set stays within the manufacturer's length and lift limits. Then comes placement. We walk the Kent home with you, weigh wall-mounted, ceiling-cassette, and floor-console options, and put each head where it throws air where you live and still looks right, with outdoor line sets covered in finished line-hide rather than left bare.

The install itself is held to the same standard as our biggest central systems. Nitrogen goes into the line set first to prove it holds pressure, then a vacuum pump pulls it down to a level verified on a micron gauge. Flares are torqued and leak-tested one by one, since that joint is where sloppy work shows up a season later. Drainage is set up with real fall or a quiet condensate pump, and the refrigerant charge is adjusted to match the line length. Finally, every zone is commissioned in both modes and you get a clear explanation of how the remote actually works. Why Professional Ductless Mini Split Installation Matters

Most failed mini splits we see in Kent trace back to installs that treated the job as simple. The flares are where it usually goes wrong: a joint that was poorly formed or never torqued to spec leaks slowly, and the homeowner finds out a season later when the head stops cooling. Moisture is the next issue, because an install that skips the nitrogen pressure test and a micron-verified vacuum leaves water vapor inside a system whose inverter compressor is sensitive to it. Overlong or overly tall line set runs sap capacity quietly, and a drain line without enough fall will drip down the wall as soon as humidity climbs.

Here is the part most installers underestimate: sizing. Even the smallest indoor heads are oversized for plenty of bedrooms, and an oversized head short-cycles, leaves moisture in the air, and never finds a steady state. Kent HVAC sizes zones honestly and installs them with the same care, torquing and testing every flare, proving the line set with nitrogen, pulling a measured vacuum, adjusting charge for the run, and testing each head in both modes. The refrigerant side is federally regulated and the electrical side calls for a properly sized circuit and disconnect, so neither is a weekend project. What you get is a system that stays silent and efficient for fifteen years instead of a rushed install that never quite works.

How to Prevent Ductless Mini Split Installation Problems

Taking preventive measures can help you avoid costly heating and cooling problems down the road. Put these technician-approved steps to work around your property:

  • Have the indoor blower wheel and coil professionally cleaned before they grow anything
  • Choose a setpoint and leave it alone, since inverters run best without constant on and off switching
  • Clean the washable filter screens in each head about once a month, since they clog faster than furnace filters
  • Clear snow, leaves, and clippings away from the outdoor unit year-round
  • Book annual professional service to verify charge, flares, and drainage while fixes are cheap
  • Winter defrost pauses are expected; heavy ice buildup on the outdoor unit is your cue to call
  • Glance at the condensate drain outlet in summer to confirm it is actually dripping
